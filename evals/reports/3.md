# how electric vehicles affect the power grid

## Introduction

Electric vehicles (EVs) offer a cleaner alternative to gas-powered vehicles regarding air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions [5]. However, rapid growth in EV adoption creates the challenge of ensuring the power grid can support new electricity demand [4]. The expected EV boom could place a major strain on the aging U.S. power grid, which was originally built for fossil fuels [3], as EVs create significant new load growth and change electric grid system dynamics [2].

## Key Findings

At scale, EVs impact electric infrastructure at the point of common coupling, which is typically the distribution system at homes, workplaces, or public stations [1]. They also affect the bulk power system as an aggregated new load, although generation and transmission systems are not expected to be impacted in the near future [1]. Because large and concentrated EV loads have a higher potential for voltage fluctuations and thermal overloads compared to smaller, dispersed loads, they require advanced planning [2]. The current grid is not fully equipped everywhere to meet the power required for anticipated EV charging increases [4], and rapidly growing EV loads will require expanded distribution capacity and long-term planning to maintain reliable electric service [4]. Without proper infrastructure upgrades, drivers may face charging difficulties such as long queues and restricted charging times and locations [3], and a strained grid becomes more vulnerable to blackouts and extreme weather events, similar to blackouts experienced in California in 2020 [3].

The specific time of day vehicles are charged is also very important [5]. If everyone charges their EVs overnight while overall electricity demand is low, supplying the extra electricity will likely have a minimal impact on the grid [5]. Conversely, charging during peak demand times like a summer afternoon creates heavy strain, meaning EVs can still indirectly cause power plant emissions [5]. Under managed charging scenarios, EV contributions to system peaks can be reduced by approximately 33 percent compared to unmanaged charging [4]. Better forecasting of EV charging needs serves as a key tool for utilities and regulators to plan for future demand through load management and proactive infrastructure investments [4]. Furthermore, managing the grid with sensors, data, modeling, and smart software controls can turn EVs into a high-value asset by incorporating energy pricing, driver preferences, and real-time charging information alongside renewable generation and grid capacity data [1]. EVs can also help grids with intermittent renewable energy by strengthening the duck curve through necessary grid storage [1]. 

The relationship between EVs and the power grid can be symbiotic, where managed transactions allow EV batteries to support and stabilize the grid during standard and emergency operations [2]. Meeting widespread EV adoption requires infrastructure investments that utilize both electricity demand and supply flexibility [2]. However, EVs and other distributed energy resources will expand the control surface of the grid, thereby increasing the complexity of operations and management [2]. To help stabilize the grid, residential solar and battery systems allow customers to generate their own power and sell excess electricity back [3]. Additionally, automakers are increasingly adding bidirectional charging capabilities so that EV batteries can power homes or supply electricity back to the grid [3]. As the electrical grid transitions to cleaner energy sources in the future, the carbon footprint of the electric cars currently on the road will continue to decrease [5].

## Conclusion

Electric vehicles introduce substantial new electricity demand and operational complexities that impact distribution systems, bulk power systems, and overall grid dynamics [1][2][4]. Without proactive infrastructure investments, advanced planning, and managed charging strategies, the EV boom risks straining aging infrastructure and increasing grid vulnerability [2][3][4]. However, through smart software controls, forecasting, energy storage, and bidirectional charging capabilities, EVs can transition from a grid stressor into a high-value asset that supports grid stability and integrates intermittent renewable energy [1][2][3].
