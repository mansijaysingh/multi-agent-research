# role of microplastics in human health

## Introduction

Microplastics (MPs) are defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 mm [2][4]. As global plastic pollution increases due to the production and breakdown of plastics, these particles spread widely through the earth's land, water, air, and food supply [2][3][4]. Because they are so pervasive, microplastics are able to infiltrate human tissues and have been discovered throughout the human body [2][3]. They are present in various organs and tissues—including the blood, saliva, liver, kidneys, lungs, brain, heart, placenta, and testicles—as well as in bodily fluids such as urine, breastmilk, and semen [3][5]. Although microplastics are recognized as a significant environmental and public health concern, research regarding their direct, long-term human impacts is still in its early stages, with much of the current focus centered on disease association and mechanisms of action [2][4][5]. Currently, there is limited evidence indicating that microplastics cause serious health problems [3].

## Key Findings

Growing data suggest that microplastics impact human biology, genes, cells, and organs [4]. Pilot studies demonstrate that microplastics and nanoplastics can enter human cells and cause major changes in gene expression, which suggests they may contribute to the progression of vascular disease [5]. Furthermore, studies have confirmed that microplastics can alter the composition of gut microbes [1]. By changing these gut microbes, microplastics modify metabolite production, a process that ultimately impacts human health [1]. 

Exposure to microscopic plastic particles also induces oxidative stress, which causes cells to upregulate antioxidant enzymes like superoxide dismutase and catalase [2]. Additionally, the human body responds to microplastic exposure through other biological systems: the immune system can adapt by modulating inflammation, while tissues utilize repair mechanisms such as cell regeneration [2]. The presence of microplastics in the body is also largely linked to disrupted hormonal regulation, which can potentially cause reproductive disorders and an increased risk of cancer [2]. 

## Conclusion

Microplastics are widely present in air, water, and food, allowing them to infiltrate human tissues and organs [2][3][5]. While current research is still in its early stages and mostly focuses on disease associations and mechanisms of action rather than direct, long-term impacts, findings show that these particles can enter human cells, alter gene expression, modify gut microbe metabolite production, and induce oxidative stress [1][2][4][5]. Researchers and governments are actively working to understand and mitigate these health risks as global plastic pollution continues to rise [3].
