# biochar adoption by smallholder farmers in Kenya

## Introduction

Smallholder farmers in Kenya face interconnected environmental and socio-economic pressures within fragile agricultural systems [1][2][3]. To address these challenges, clean energy initiatives have introduced biochar to improve soil conditions while supporting local environmental and socio-economic needs [2][3]. A qualitative study conducted in Kenya's Embu and Kwale counties explored how smallholder farmers understand sustainability, perceive biochar, and navigate adoption barriers using interviews and focus groups [1]. Additionally, research investigated household biochar production and use among 150 smallholder farmers across three locations who produce biochar using locally manufactured GASTOV Top-lit Updraft gasifier cookstoves [4]. Further studies have evaluated maize grain yield responses to biochar produced from locally available biomass wastes like coconut shells, coffee husks, and maize cobs [5].

## Key Findings

Farmers understand sustainability as continuity and resilience, viewing the economy as a tool to support environmental goals [2][3]. Smallholder farmers value biochar for several key benefits, including improved soil fertility, higher crop yields, better water retention, lower fertilizer costs, cleaner household energy, and reduced pest pressure [1]. Biochar also serves as a symbolic source of hope, a way to gain a sense of control, and a tool that boosts resilience within these communities [1][2][3]. Furthermore, experienced users rely on practical trials, new users depend on community narratives, and youth see biochar as an opportunity for modernization [3].

In household production measurements across 75 households during the cooking of a common Kenyan meal, findings from Kwale County showed an average fuel savings of 18% compared to a traditional three-stone open fire [4]. During this cooking process, 200 grams of biochar were produced, accounting for 16.5% of the biomass used, and the material was tested in field trials with maize and kale at doses of 1 to 10 tonnes per hectare [4]. When biochar was applied along plant rows at rates of 1, 5, and 10 Mg ha−1 in maize trials, grain yields consistently increased by an average of 1.0, 2.6, and 4.0 Mg ha−1 above the control for the respective application rates [5]. Because the yield response decreased with higher application rates due to diminishing returns, spreading a given amount of biochar over a larger area optimizes productivity [5].

Despite these agricultural and environmental benefits, biochar adoption remains limited [1]. Adoption is influenced by social, cultural, emotional, and institutional factors such as gender norms, resource limits, and training access [1]. Additionally, a generally positive disposition toward biochar may have caused a positive selection bias by overrepresenting benefits and missing skeptical voices [1]. 

## Conclusion

Locally produced biochar emerges as a promising method for enhancing agricultural production, carbon storage, and clean household energy on smallholder farms across diverse Kenyan conditions [1][4][5]. Farmers view it as a valuable agricultural input that improves soil fertility, increases crop yields, and fosters resilience and hope [1][2][3]. However, because adoption is constrained by institutional, social, cultural, and resource-related barriers, and because findings remain context-specific to the studied counties, caution is needed when applying these results to other regions [1].
