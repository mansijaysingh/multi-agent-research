# biochar adoption by smallholder farmers in Kenya

## Introduction

Smallholder farmers in Kenya, who cultivate under five acres, serve as the primary target market for biochar adoption [1]. These resource-constrained farmers face severe environmental and socio-economic pressures, including soil degradation, erratic rainfall, prolonged droughts, and seasonal floods [3]. In Kenya, biochar largely emerged through energy access initiatives [5]. When applied to farms, biochar acts as a soil amendment that improves water retention, soil structure, nutrient retention, microbial activity, and long-term soil stability [5]. Furthermore, companies like BIOSORRA produce biochar through pyrolysis at temperatures between 550 and 700°C, which locks carbon into a stable form that can remain in the soil for over a thousand years [4]. A 2025 qualitative study by Chanda Poudyal investigates how farmers in Embu and Kwale counties perceive sustainability and biochar adoption using focus group discussions and key informant interviews [3].

## Key Findings

Despite having limited capital, smallholder farmers are motivated to adopt biochar because it improves soil fertility and crop yields [1]. Farmers prioritize agricultural solutions that deliver measurable returns within a single growing season [1]. Cooperative structures help overcome individual financial barriers to enable broader participation in the market [1]. Additionally, organizations that provide comprehensive support—including financing, technical assistance, and quality products—are positioned to capture market leadership [1]. 

A transdisciplinary research project involving 150 farming households across three locations demonstrated that farmers could produce biochar using locally manufactured GASTOV Top-lit Updraft gasifier cookstoves and apply it to their fields [2]. Measurements during the cooking of a common Kenyan meal showed fuel savings and biochar production potential [2]. Specifically, findings from Kwale County demonstrated an average fuel savings of 18% compared to a traditional three-stone open fire while producing 200 grams of biochar, which equaled 16.5% of the used biomass [2]. Farmers used this biochar in field trials for maize and kale, comparing it against normal farming practices at doses of 1 to 10 tonnes per hectare [2]. 

Soil amendments produced by companies like BIOSORRA help improve water retention, nutrient holding, and crop yields, and are applied either as a stand-alone conditioner or as carefully designed blends with local organic nutrient sources tailored to regional soil conditions and crop needs [4]. However, smallholder farmers in Kenya often lack access to financing mechanisms and certification systems for biochar, and both Kenya and Colombia currently lack clear and comprehensive regulatory frameworks governing it [5]. Without proper safeguards, scaling biochar risks deepening existing inequalities within communities [5]. Moreover, without understanding local perspectives, dissemination efforts may remain top-down and poorly aligned with realities, thereby limiting meaningful uptake [3].

## Conclusion

Rural African biochar systems can help address food and energy security challenges for farmers, support sustainable development, and contribute to climate change mitigation [2][3]. BIOSORRA operates in full compliance with Kenya's environmental regulator, NEMA, after completing an Environmental Impact Assessment [4]. Ultimately, rural biochar systems and local perceptions of sustainability must be carefully understood to ensure successful adoption and climate mitigation without exacerbating existing community inequalities [2][3][5].
