# pros and cons of nuclear energy for climate goals

## Introduction

Nuclear energy harnesses the power of nuclear reactions to generate electricity in order to meet global energy demand [1]. Proponents argue that nuclear power helps reduce carbon emissions and mitigate climate change because it offers a low carbon footprint compared to traditional fossil fuels like gas and oil [1][2]. However, the use of nuclear energy in achieving climate goals involves a complex debate regarding its environmental benefits, economic drawbacks, and operational challenges.

## Key Findings

When evaluating nuclear energy for climate goals, several advantages emerge. Nuclear power plants provide a steady, reliable, and stable base load supply of electricity that does not rely on weather conditions, unlike intermittent renewable sources like solar and wind [1][2]. This reliable supply can assist while the energy grid infrastructure is adapted [2]. Furthermore, nuclear energy has a high energy density, allowing a small amount of fuel to produce a substantial amount of energy efficiently [1]. It also requires less land than renewable energy sources [2][3][5] and occupies a relatively small land area [4]. While considered a carbon-free electricity source during operation that does not produce air pollution [4], nuclear plants actually produce about 117 grams of CO2 per kilowatt-hour over their life cycle [2][3][5]. This is lower than traditional fossil fuels like natural gas [3], oil, and gas [2][5], but higher than renewable sources such as onshore wind at 9 grams and solar energy at 33 grams [2]. Additionally, fleets of small modular reactors (SMRs) create an even higher overall carbon footprint per kilowatt-hour [3].

Despite these benefits, significant drawbacks and challenges exist. Detractors raise concerns regarding safety risks, potential catastrophic accidents, and the long-term management of radioactive waste [1]. Safety concerns already exist regarding third-generation nuclear reactors in practice, and plant operations and waste management pose environmental and health challenges [3][4]. Economically, huge investments are needed for the energy transition, and focusing these massive funds on nuclear power instead of renewables does not make economic sense [2][5]. Nuclear plants also feature very high upfront costs [4]. Furthermore, processes like mining and refining uranium consume energy [4], and uranium is technically a non-renewable energy source [4]. Finally, climate change itself threatens the industry because water-intensive inland nuclear plants use a lot of water, which can cause or worsen localized water stress and competition for water use [2][3][5]. Additionally, turning reactors on and off to match energy needs puts stress on materials [5].

## Conclusion

Nuclear energy offers a reliable, stable base load supply with a relatively small land footprint and a lower carbon footprint than traditional fossil fuels [1][2][3][4][5]. These attributes allow it to support energy grids during the climate transition [2]. However, its lifecycle emissions are higher than wind and solar, and the sector faces severe hurdles, including very high upfront costs, economic disadvantages compared to renewables, radioactive waste management challenges, safety and accident risks, and vulnerabilities to climate-induced water scarcity [1][2][3][4][5].
