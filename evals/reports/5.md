# pros and cons of nuclear energy for climate goals

## Introduction

As global discussions on climate change intensify, the role of nuclear energy in meeting climate goals has become a subject of significant debate. Nuclear energy currently provides about 20% of the nation's electricity and represents a substantial part of the world energy mix, with its use expected to grow in the coming decades [1][5]. This research report examines the pros and cons of utilizing nuclear energy to achieve climate goals based on current operational, environmental, economic, and safety factors.

## Key Findings

Proponents highlight several major advantages of nuclear energy in the context of climate change mitigation. Nuclear energy is considered a clean power source because it produces no carbon pollution or greenhouse gas emissions during operation, with plant cooling towers releasing only water vapor [1][3]. It serves as a direct substitute for fossil fuels and is most effective as part of a low-carbon energy mix when phasing out these fuels [2]. Furthermore, nuclear power provides reliable baseload energy, high energy output with a small physical footprint, and existing infrastructure in some areas [1]. Beyond electricity, it offers a carbon-free source of heat that could potentially power industrial processes such as the manufacturing of concrete and steel [2]. France successfully utilized nuclear energy to cut its emissions instead of fossil fuels [3]. From a public health perspective, nuclear power is much safer than fossil fuels—which are responsible for one in five deaths worldwide—and holds the lowest mortality rate per unit of energy generated when compared to coal, natural gas, solar, wind, and hydro [2][3]. Additionally, up to 90% of nuclear waste can be recycled and reused, rendering it significantly less dangerous than the toxic chemicals produced by fossil fuels [3], and future prospects are supported by advancements in small modular reactors and next-generation designs [1]. 

Conversely, significant drawbacks and risks complicate the deployment of nuclear energy for climate goals. A major disadvantage is that nuclear plants are more expensive to build than solar or wind farms, particularly on a small scale [2]. New conventional nuclear power costs about five times more per kilowatt-hour than onshore wind power, and nuclear projects take significantly longer to plan and operate while producing higher emissions per unit of electricity than wind power [4]. Because the United States requires near-term solutions to reduce carbon pollution, high costs and long turnaround times mean nuclear energy functions more as a long-term solution [1]. Conventional nuclear energy also presents multiple economic and security risks, including the risk of nuclear weapons proliferation acting as a barrier to expansion, as noted by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Knowledge (IPCC) [4]. Finally, the nuclear fuel cycle generates dangers related to meltdowns, lung cancer risks from mining, and radioactive waste management [4].

## Conclusion

Nuclear energy offers powerful advantages for climate goals, including carbon-free operation, reliable baseload capacity, a carbon-free heat source for industrial processes, and a strong safety record regarding human mortality relative to fossil fuels. However, its high capital costs, prolonged planning and construction timelines, weapon proliferation risks, and fuel-cycle dangers present notable obstacles. While it is essential for sustainable economic growth and mitigating climate change impacts in the long term, its viability requires weighing these substantial economic and security disadvantages against its environmental benefits [4][5].
